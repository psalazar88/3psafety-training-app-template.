## 1. Bottom line

**Nothing in these photographs should be lifted with, pulled with, or stood near until it has been inspected hands-on by a qualified person.**

The assembly shows the classic signature of equipment that has been parked outdoors and forgotten: pervasive corrosion on every load-bearing component, an unreadable warning decal, no legible capacity or identification marking anywhere on the item or the rope, and a multi-thousand-pound steel mass sitting on crushed wooden shipping pallets on uneven gravel.

Two conditions are, on their face, **immediate removal-from-service** conditions under OSHA and ASME criteria and do not require any further investigation to act on:

1. The **wire rope assembly** is corroded over its full visible length, including at its end fittings, and carries **no identification tag**.
2. The **item itself carries no legible rated-load marking**, so no one can establish a safe working load for it — which makes any planned lift or pull unverifiable by definition.

Separately, and independent of whether this gear is ever used again, the **storage arrangement is an active crush and tip-over hazard right now** to anyone walking past it.

---

## 2. What is in the photographs

A heavy fabricated/cast steel **U-shaped (horseshoe) frame or slab**, roughly estimated at 5–6 ft long and 3–3.5 ft wide by scale against the 48-inch pallet beneath it. Features visible:

- A rounded, chamfered nose at the closed end of the "U"; traces of original **yellow paint** survive on the lower flanges.
- Two raised longitudinal rails along the inner edges, each carrying the repeated cast marking **"MERCO"**.
- Two long rectangular pockets flanking the central slot, consistent with **fork pockets** for forklift handling.
- Several large circular recessed pads on the top face (consistent with stacking/alignment pockets or cored weight pockets).
- A recessed cast **name plaque** on the top face near the nose — present but **not legible** in any of the supplied photographs.
- At one rear corner, a **bolted-on lug plate** secured by four studs and nuts, carrying a yellow **DANGER decal**, and mounting a **swivel/clevis shackle fitting**.
- A **wire rope** shackled to that fitting, running off across the gravel and terminating in a free, unattached eye on the ground.

**Positive identification was not possible.** The item may be a below-the-hook lifting device, a counterweight/ballast slab, or a machine attachment. That ambiguity is itself finding **F-01** — a piece of heavy gear that no one on site can positively identify and rate is, operationally, a piece of gear that cannot be safely used.

Estimated mass, from scale alone, is **well over 1,000 lb and plausibly in the 2,000–4,000 lb range**. The actual weight is unknown and is not marked on the item.

## 4. Detailed notes

### 4.1 The wire rope (F-02, F-03, F-10, F-11)

The rope is uniformly rust-brown from the shackle to its free end, with no trace of lubricant and no bright wire anywhere. Two swaged sleeves are visible along the length; at one of them a wire end appears to protrude at the sleeve edge. Corrosion is heaviest exactly where it matters most — at and under the end fittings, where it cannot be inspected without disassembly and where it hides broken wires.

This is enough on its own. OSHA's wire-rope-sling removal list treats **corrosion of the rope or end attachments** as a condemning condition; a broken-wire count is not required before pulling it. Add the missing identification tag and there is no lawful basis on which anyone could put this rope into service.

The eye bears directly on the shackle bow with no thimble, and the free end simply lies in the gravel — neither stowed nor connected to anything. That it is dragged across an abrasive surface means grit is working into the strands, which degrades a rope from the inside out where inspection cannot see it.

**Action: cut and scrap.** Do not re-tag, do not "clean it up and see." Cutting it prevents it quietly returning to service.

### 4.2 The shackle and bolted lug (F-04, F-08, F-09)

Under high magnification the clevis, pin and shackle body show heavy scale and surface pitting across every face. No size, grade or working-load-limit stamp can be read. ASME B30.26 treats illegible identification on rigging hardware as a removal condition, precisely because unmarked hardware invites someone to assume a rating it may never have had.

The pull lug is **bolted on** rather than welded or integral. That may well be the manufacturer's design — but from the photos there is no way to establish fastener grade, whether the correct washers are fitted, or whether the joint has ever been torqued or re-torqued. The exposed thread above each nut is heavily corroded and deformed. If this lug is used to pull or lift, it is a custom lifting accessory and OSHA expects it to be marked with a safe working load and proof tested to 125% of that load.

**Action: quarantine the fitting with the item. Do not re-use the shackle on other gear.**

### 4.3 The warning decal (F-07)

Only the signal word "DANGER" and the outline of a pictogram panel survive. The hazard statement, the consequence and the avoidance instruction are gone. A warning that cannot be read is not a warning — and in an incident investigation, a decal in this condition tends to become evidence that the equipment was not being maintained rather than evidence that the worker was warned.

**Action: obtain the correct replacement decal from the OEM as part of any return-to-service work. Do not substitute a generic sticker.**

### 4.4 The storage arrangement (F-05, F-06)

This is the finding that can hurt someone **today**, regardless of what happens to the rigging.

A steel mass conservatively over 1,000 lb — and plausibly several times that — is resting on used wooden shipping pallets. The photographs show the deck boards split and crushed where the steel edge bears on them, and the pallet partially collapsed. Part of the item sits on the pallet and part on loose gravel, with overhang, on an unlevel surface, with no cribbing or blocking anywhere.

A standard stringer pallet is rated in the low thousands of pounds only when the load is **uniformly distributed and fully supported on a flat floor**. Here the load is a concentrated edge load on a weathered pallet of unknown history, on gravel. The remaining margin is unknown and the pallet is already failing visibly.

If it lets go, the item drops and can slide or roll. Anyone walking past — and the rope lying across the ground invites people to walk past — is in the line of fire.

**Action, today:** barricade the item, keep people clear, and re-set it flat on the ground or on properly sized hardwood cribbing using equipment rated for the load. Removing a partially collapsed pallet from under a multi-thousand-pound object is itself a planned lift with a line-of-fire exclusion zone — not a job for a pry bar and a couple of people.

## 5. What could not be determined from photographs

State these plainly in any onward use of this report. They are the reason this is an assessment and not an inspection.

- Actual rope diameter, construction, class and **broken wires per lay** — requires hands-on inspection along the full length.
- Whether section loss from corrosion has reduced any component below its design thickness.
- Whether the frame, welds, lug or fork pockets contain **cracks** — requires cleaning to bare metal and, at these load levels, likely NDT.
- Fastener grade, washer fitment and joint torque on the bolted lug.
- Whether the item is a rated lifting device at all, versus a counterweight or machine attachment.
- Whether any **proof test, periodic inspection, or maintenance records** exist for the item or the rope.
- Whether the item has an owner, a designated user, or any place in a current inspection programme.

## 6. Recommended actions

### Immediate — before end of shift

1. **Barricade** the item and maintain an exclusion zone; treat the crushed pallet as a live collapse hazard (F-05, F-06).
2. **Red-tag** the item, the shackle and the rope as OUT OF SERVICE — DO NOT USE (F-12).
3. **Cut and scrap the wire rope assembly.** Removing it from service by destruction is the only reliable way to keep it out (F-02, F-03).
4. **Coil and remove nothing else by hand** until the item is stabilised; clear the rope end off the walking surface as part of step 3 (F-11).

### Within 7 days

5. **Stabilise the item** — a planned lift using equipment rated for the unknown-but-significant weight, setting it down flat on grade or on sized cribbing.
6. **Establish identity and rating.** Clean the cast plaque back to legible metal and photograph it; contact the OEM with the "MERCO" marking and the frame dimensions to obtain the data plate information, rated load and manual.
7. **Decide the item's disposition.** If it cannot be positively identified and rated, it does not go back into service as lifting gear — full stop. Scrap it or reclassify it as non-lifting ballast with permanent marking that says so.
8. **If return to service is pursued:** competent-person inspection with the frame cleaned to bare metal, weld and lug examination, replacement of the shackle with new marked hardware, new OEM decals, proof test per 29 CFR 1926.251(a)(4) / ASME B30.20, and entry into the periodic inspection register.

### Systemic — the reason this happened

9. **Rigging register and periodic inspection.** Every sling, shackle and lifting attachment in the yard needs a unique ID, a rated capacity, and a periodic inspection on a defined interval. A single unmarked, uninspected item in a yard means the programme is not closed.
10. **Yard sweep.** Photograph and inventory every other piece of rigging in this laydown area. Equipment in this condition is rarely alone — the same storage practice that produced this item produced its neighbours.
11. **Storage standard.** Define how heavy gear is laid down: on grade or on rated cribbing, never on shipping pallets; rigging stored off the ground and under cover.
12. **Retrain on pre-use inspection.** Two of the conditions here (no tag, visible corrosion) are ones a trained user is expected to catch in seconds at the point of use. That they persisted suggests pre-use inspection is not happening or is not being acted on.

## 8. Limitations

This assessment is based solely on four photographs supplied on 17 September 2026. No site visit was made, no component was handled, measured or tested, and no maintenance, inspection or purchase records were reviewed. Conditions that are stated as observed are those visible in the images at high magnification; everything else is explicitly listed in Section 5 as undetermined.

Findings are referenced to the US standards an employer would most likely be assessed against. They are offered to direct a competent-person inspection, and are **not** a determination of regulatory compliance or non-compliance. The removal-from-service recommendations in Section 6 are conservative by design: where a photograph cannot rule out a condemning condition, the equipment is treated as condemned until a hands-on inspection says otherwise.
